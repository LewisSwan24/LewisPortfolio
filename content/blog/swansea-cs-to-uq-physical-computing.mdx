Studying Computer Science at Swansea University (2020–2024, graduating Upper Second-Class Honours 2:1) trains a specific engineering reflex: long before polishing an interface, ask what breaks under bad input, concurrency collisions, network partitions, or power loss. Writing an extended undergraduate honours dissertation reinforced the same discipline—an argument holds only to the boundary of what its implementation can actually prove.

Transitioning into the Master of Interaction Design at The University of Queensland (2025–2026) initially felt like stepping out of strict determinism into messy human interviews. Yet once 2025 studio coursework and 2026 physical-computing builds forced software rules into real tables, wrists, and noisy rooms, both trainings locked together.

## What pure CS abstractions miss in the room

A clean desktop state machine assumes an operator seated at a desk with both eyes fixed on a crisp monitor. Put four strangers around a wooden table in [MazeRunners](/projects/maze), or put a developer three meters away from their workstation with [Walkie](/projects/walkie), and textbook assumptions collapse into three physical realities:

1. **Posture sets cognitive bandwidth**: At our public exhibition across 9 groups (36 players), almost nobody watched the digital scoreboard while guiding the steel ball—eyes stayed locked on the physical wood until sleeve haptics buzzed under their fingertips.
2. **Firmware timing constants are UX rules**: A `45 ms` FC-51 infrared stabilization check and a `1200 ms` per-zone cooldown read like embedded constants, yet without them a double-bounce makes players suspect the whole scoring system is broken.
3. **Constructive friction prevents automation regret**: Locking risky card focus onto `'Not now'` in [Lamplight Nook](/projects/lamplight), separating Front Approve from Side Deny on [Walkie](/projects/walkie), or keeping open ATS answers blank in [Resume Autofill](/projects/autofill) applies state-machine safety directly to human habit loops.

## How 2025 foundations bridged into 2026 shipped systems

During my first year at UQ in 2025, structured think-aloud testing, paper-first information architecture redraws, WCAG browser injection experiments, and scoped AI consent rules reshaped how I approach problem framing. Rather than diving straight into component code or relying on Swansea-era classroom exercises, I focused my public portfolio squarely on high-conviction builds from the past one to two years—systems I could test end to end in browser, desktop, extension, and embedded runtimes.

That bridge carries directly into how I build as a Design Engineer today:

- **Clear ownership boundaries with AI coding tools**: Generated code serves as an accelerator after state transitions, Zod contracts, and refusal limits are defined—never as unquestioned truth.
- **Reproducible engineering gates**: Every shipped case in [the Archive Vault](/vault) and [Protocol Replay Studio](/studio/replay) distinguishes real hardware/code verification (such as `gate-h`, `gate-p5`, `gate-l`, or `430/430` unit checks) from outside participant testing still left to run.
- **Bilingual parity without glossing over limits**: Designing and engineering across both English and Chinese teams keeps terminology honest, crisp, and verifiable.
