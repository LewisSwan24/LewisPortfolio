Studying Computer Science at Swansea University (2020–2024) trains a specific reflex: before building a feature, ask what breaks under concurrency, bad input, or power loss. Transitioning into the Master of Interaction Design at The University of Queensland (2025–2026) initially felt like stepping out of strict invariants into ambiguous human behavior—until physical computing bridged both worlds.

## Where pure CS abstractions stop holding

A clean state machine on a desktop monitor assumes an operator looking directly at the screen with both hands on a keyboard. Put four strangers around a wooden table in [MazeRunners](/projects/maze), or put a developer three meters away from their workstation with [Walkie](/projects/walkie), and three non-negotiable realities replace textbook assumptions:

1. **Physical posture dictates cognitive bandwidth**: Exhibition players never read the scoring monitor while the steel ball was rolling—their eyes stayed glued to the wood until finger-sleeve haptics fired.
2. **Debounce thresholds are UX decisions**: A `45 ms` FC-51 infrared gate and `1200 ms` zone cooldown are firmware constants, yet they determine whether a player trusts the score or assumes the table is broken.
3. **Refusal beats convenience when mistakes cost real state**: Defaulting a risk card to `'Not now'` in [Lamplight Nook](/projects/lamplight) or reserving wrist approvals for a perpendicular Front Key is state-machine safety applied to human muscle memory.

## Why both degrees live in every build

My CS foundation ensures prototypes run on real silicon, typed schemas, and reproducible build gates rather than disposable clickable slides. My UQ design training ensures those engineering guarantees target an actual human tension—and state honestly where user observation stopped.
