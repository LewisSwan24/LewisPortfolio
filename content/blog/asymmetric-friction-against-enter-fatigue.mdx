Standard desktop UX treats symmetry as polish: *"Cancel"* on the left, a bright *"Continue"* button focused by default on the right. That works for saving a draft. My argument is that it fails when an autonomous agent asks to overwrite files or run shell commands.

In [Lamplight Nook](/projects/lamplight), [Walkie](/projects/walkie) and [Resume Autofill](/projects/autofill), I aimed for **asymmetric friction**: cheap to pause, deliberate to commit. All three are personal projects whose code was mostly written by AI coding tools (Claude Code); I set the rules, reviewed the output and verified it, and so far only I have used them.

## Making refusal the path of least resistance

1. **Default-safe focus ([Lamplight Nook](/projects/lamplight))**: Lamplight Nook 1.0 was released on 5 November 2026. On risky cards, initial keyboard focus lands on **Not now**, and pressing `Enter` or `Escape` out of habit means "Not now" rather than "go ahead".
2. **Key asymmetry ([Walkie](/projects/walkie))**: holding the front key records speech, but a transcript can never approve a tool call. A permission prompt waits on the watch screen until a short front-key press (allow) or side-key press (deny); holding the front key denies and records a spoken reason.
3. **Human submit ([Resume Autofill](/projects/autofill))**: the extension can press "Next" on multi-page forms, marks uncertain fields with dashed (needs review) or thick solid (not matched) outlines, and never presses the final Submit.

## Friction that preserves agency without lecturing

Asymmetric friction does not scold the user with three confirmation pop-ups. It makes sure that pressing `Enter` on reflex, or looking away, lands on the safest reversible outcome. Try the patterns in the [Pattern Atlas](/patterns).
