The first input idea for [MazeRunners](/projects/maze) was buttons and a joystick. Our course paper records why it was dropped: it felt too much like an arcade game. The version we built wires each tilt direction to a pair of same-coloured conductive wristbands, so no single player can move the board alone.

## What the exhibition record shows

At the June 2026 exhibition, 9 groups of four (36 players) played, and about 3 groups finished. The handwritten score sheet was not kept, so there is no timing data. The notes we do have record three things:

1. **The coupling was hard at first.** A team member explained the rules before play, and many players still struggled at first to match their colour to the right move.
2. **The talk came from the wiring.** The calls we noted were short: "wait", "stop", "left", "right", "don't touch the trap", "you two move now". Groups with a clear leader moved more smoothly; when all four competed for the call, they hit more traps or dropped the ball.
3. **The feedback was stronger than planned.** The finger-sleeve vibration was stronger than we expected, and some players were startled the first time it fired.

## A sensor detail behind it

The ten FC-51 infrared sensors swung with distance, angle and venue lighting. A three-sample debounce and a 400 ms cooldown made one pass of the ball count once. The [Protocol Replay Studio](/studio/replay?track=maze) steps through a hand-authored simulation of that logic, not captured hardware data.

## Carrying the idea into wearables

The same aim, pulling attention back to the room, shaped [On The Beat](/projects/onthebeat): when two people end a meeting with LET'S FINISH, the card they met on goes off. Claude Code wrote most of On The Beat's code; I set the rules, reviewed the output and checked it on two wristbands. Nobody has worn it at a gig yet, so this is a design intent, not a result.
