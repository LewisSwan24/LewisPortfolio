Method guides usually read as a menu: here are twelve techniques, here is when to use each. That framing is not wrong, but it skips the part that actually matters — what a given method structurally *cannot* tell you, and what you therefore still have to go and find some other way.

On [UQ Connect](/projects/connect) I restructured a university portal whose top level was twelve categories named after the departments that owned them: HiQ, SI-net, mySI-net, ask.UQ. I used three methods. Each answered exactly one question, and the most useful finding came from none of them.

## Think-aloud: where people give up

I ran task-based sessions on the existing portal and watched where people stopped trying. The most quoted moment:

> "I just Google 'UQ how to drop a course'. I'd never look for it in the portal."

That is not a navigation problem you fix by relabelling. It says the portal had already lost, and users had routed around it entirely. Think-aloud is good at exactly this: it shows the moment of abandonment, which no amount of structural analysis will surface — because structural analysis assumes people are still inside the structure.

What it cannot do is tell you what the structure should be instead. Five people giving up in five different places does not add up to an architecture.

## Open card sort: the users' own grouping

So: an open card sort, where participants grouped the portal's common tasks and named the groups themselves. Affinity mapping across the results clustered around five life stages — arriving, studying, belonging, supporting myself, moving on.

That gave the shape. Twelve department-owned categories collapsed into six task-led ones, with "I'm new" promoted to the front door, because arriving is the stage with the least existing knowledge and the most urgency.

What a card sort cannot do is tell you whether the resulting tree works. It is a generative method producing a hypothesis — and it is very easy to fall in love with a hypothesis you watched real users build with their own hands.

## Tree test: does the structure survive contact

So the candidate structures went into an unmoderated tree test against the original: no visual design, no search, just the hierarchy and a set of tasks. This is the only one of the three that produces a comparison rather than an anecdote.

It also earned the decision I am most pleased with, which was a *hedge*. The winning tree kept an A–Z index as a secondary route. Task-led navigation is better for people who do not know the vocabulary and worse for people who do — the returning student who knows the thing is called SI-net and wants it in one hop. Keeping both is not indecision; it serves two populations the tree test could show separately.

## The one that caught the most

Every category name went to two readers with no University of Queensland background for a plain-language review. Cost: an afternoon. It caught four names that sounded fine to anyone inside the institution and meant nothing outside it.

None of the three "real" methods could have caught those, and it is worth being precise about why. Card-sort participants were students — they had already absorbed the institutional vocabulary, so a name that is only decodable from the inside reads as perfectly clear to them. Think-aloud drew on the same population. And a tree test measures whether people reach the right node, not whether the label would mean anything to someone who has not arrived yet — which, for a portal whose front door is "I'm new", is precisely the audience that matters most.

The blind spot was shared across all three methods, because it came from the sample rather than from the technique.

## What only showed up at higher fidelity

One more, because it argues against testing structure in isolation forever. "Money" and "My account" tested fine as tree nodes, and became genuinely confusable once they sat on a screen together. Renamed, retested, gone. Some problems are not architecture problems — they only exist once things are adjacent.

## The honest boundary

This was coursework. The IA was tested, not deployed. I can walk through how the card sort was grouped, how the tree-test tasks were written, and why each label changed. I have never measured what it did to real search behaviour or support-ticket volume, because it never went live. That is a genuine limit on what the project proves, and I would rather state it than let a reader assume otherwise.

## The actual takeaway

Choosing a method is less about matching a technique to a question than about knowing what each one is blind to, and covering that blind spot with something else — often something much cheaper.

Three methods told me where people gave up, what grouping matched their mental model, and which of two structures performed better. All three shared one blind spot, because all three drew on the same population. Two outsiders and an afternoon covered it.
