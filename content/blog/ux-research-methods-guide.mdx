Method guides usually read as a menu: here are twelve techniques, here is when to use each. That framing is not wrong, but it skips the part that actually matters — what a given method structurally *cannot* tell you, and what you therefore still have to go and find some other way.

On [UQ Connect](/projects/connect) — a semester-long team project building a social platform for international students — we ran three rounds of research between August and November. We also set up more measurement apparatus than most student projects ever touch. The finding that changed the product most came from none of it, and cost nothing.

## We wrote the pass mark first

Before a single interview, every hypothesis got a written threshold. Not "we think students disengage when they are tired," but:

> We are right if ≥70% report that participation tracks weekly energy and load, and the logs show a negative correlation at `p<.05`.

Four hypotheses, four thresholds. One of them planned to use UCLA-3 for loneliness and WHO-5 for wellbeing, looking for r ≤ −0.4 between the two.

I still think this was the right instinct, and I would do it again. The whole point of writing a threshold down is that it can fail. Without one, whatever the interviews return can be read as support — you go looking for quotes that fit, and interviews are generous enough to supply them.

## What the interviews actually killed

Thirty-three transcripts in the first round, across five interviewers, between 15 and 30 August. Two assumptions did not survive.

The first was that language proficiency was the barrier. It was not. The same students, at the same level of English, went quiet in fast, loud, crowded rooms and spoke freely in small, slow ones. The barrier is pace and group size — which is a completely different product problem, because you cannot fix proficiency but you can absolutely fix format.

The second was that the gap was exposure. It was follow-through. People found the events fine. They still did not go.

Both are written down in the learning cards as things we got wrong, which is the only reason I can tell you about them now.

## Think-aloud: where people give up

Second round, September, on a prototype that already ran. A participant clicking through campus events, thinking out loud:

> it shows I'm attending this. Oh wait, but it's still clickable, like attending and joining. And this button kind of disturbing.

> Not sure what the one button behind was doing. Maybe chat to someone.

> Can I go to the previous page? How can I do that?

> Skill swap. Share your skills. Not sure what that is.

Four defects in one session. None of them needs a validated scale to detect, and none of them would have surfaced in a demo, because in a demo the person driving already knows where everything is.

This is what think-aloud is uniquely good at: the moment of abandonment. Structural analysis cannot see it, because structural analysis assumes the person is still inside the structure.

What it cannot do is tell you what to build instead. Four people stuck in four places is not a design.

## The response was structural, so the answer had to be

On 3 October we stopped patching and redrew the whole application on one sheet of paper: five screens, an arrow for every route off the home page, and one person's name against each feature area.

That is the decision I am most pleased with, and it is not a clever one. It is just the recognition that "there is no way back" and "this card contradicts its own button" are not component bugs. You cannot fix a missing return path one screen at a time. The November build started from that sheet.

## What the expensive apparatus never produced

Here is the part I would rather state than let a reader assume.

We wrote the thresholds. We never formally scored the transcripts against them. There is no table anywhere in that repository showing hypothesis one passing at 74% and hypothesis three failing at 31%. What I have is a set of qualitative patterns that happen to point in the direction the thresholds anticipated — which is precisely the failure mode pre-registration is supposed to prevent.

So the honest scoreboard: the pre-written thresholds shaped what we asked, and kept us from quietly reframing misses as findings. They did not, in the end, produce a number. The cheap method produced four fixable defects in an afternoon.

## The honest boundary

This was a course project with five people. I ran 14 of the 71 interviews and owned the front-end component system and the shared state layer; the map, the wellness hub and the matching logic were other people's work.

The MVP runs on mock data. The backend was written and never connected, so matching, messaging and scheduling have never met a real record, a concurrent user, or an empty state. And no round recorded completion times or a fixed success criterion — which means I can tell you the September navigation defect did not recur in the November task chain, and I cannot tell you by how much anything improved.

## The actual takeaway

Choosing a method is less about matching a technique to a question than about knowing what each one is blind to, and covering that blind spot with something else — often something much cheaper.

Set the pass mark in advance anyway. It disciplines the question even when it does not produce the number. But do not mistake the apparatus for the evidence: the thing that moved this product was one person, one prototype, and the question *how do I go back?*
