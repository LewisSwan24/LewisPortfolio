Method guides usually read as a menu: here are twelve techniques, here is when to use each. That framing is not wrong, but it skips the part that matters most: what a given method structurally *cannot* tell you, and what you therefore still have to find some other way.

On **UQ Connect**, a semester-long team project building a social platform for international students, five of us ran three rounds of research between August and November 2025. We also wrote more measurement into the plan than most student projects do. As the end of this post explains, most of it was never used. The clearest defects came from the cheapest method.

## We wrote the pass mark first

Before a single interview, every hypothesis got a written threshold. Not "we think students disengage when they are tired," but:

> We are right if ≥70% report that participation tracks weekly energy and load, and the logs show a negative correlation at `p<.05`.

Thirteen hypotheses, thirteen thresholds: eight about the problem space, written before we spoke to anyone, and five about specific MVP features, written before we prototyped them. One threshold named UCLA-3 for loneliness and WHO-5 for wellbeing, looking for r ≤ −0.4 between the two. Those scales were only written into the threshold; we never administered them.

I still think writing thresholds down was the right instinct. The point of a threshold is that it can fail. Without one, whatever the interviews return can be read as support: you go looking for quotes that fit, and interviews are generous enough to supply them.

## Where the interviews pointed away from us

The first round produced thirty-three transcripts between 15 and 30 August. The team's learning cards record two places where what we heard pointed away from our assumptions. We did not score this formally, so these are qualitative patterns, not falsified hypotheses.

The first was language. We had assumed proficiency was the barrier; the learning card puts it as "barriers are about pace/size more than absolute proficiency". That is a different product problem, because you cannot change someone's proficiency, but you can change the format of an event.

The second was exposure. We had assumed students did not know about events; the card records that the gap was follow-through. People found the events. They still did not go.

## And two the thresholds could never have caught

Thirteen hypotheses went in. Fourteen learning cards came out. Twelve map back to a hypothesis. Two do not.

The first is **cost**. Event fees of $100–200 kept people out. One participant said events charging $150–200 are too expensive. Not one of our thirteen hypotheses mentioned money.

The second is **continuity**. Clubs recruit at Market Day and then go quiet, so signing up never becomes attending. What keeps people is visible, ongoing activity.

Neither came from a hypothesis, and that is the argument against treating pre-registration as the whole method. A threshold can only test a question you already thought to ask. The process still needs something that lets the unasked question in.

## Think-aloud: where people give up

Second round, September, on a prototype that already ran. A participant clicking through campus events, thinking out loud:

> it shows I'm attending this. Oh wait, but it's still clickable, like attending and joining. And this button kind of disturbing.

> Not sure what the one button behind was doing. Maybe chat to someone.

> Can I go to the previous page? How can I do that?

> Skill swap. Share your skills. Not sure what that is.

Four defects in one session. None of them needs a validated scale to detect, and none would have surfaced in a demo, because in a demo the person driving already knows where everything is.

This is what think-aloud is good at: the moment of abandonment. What it cannot do is tell you what to build instead. Four people stuck in four places is not a design.

## Back to paper

On 3 October the team went back to paper and redrew the structure on one sheet: five screens, an arrow for every route off the home page, and one person's name against each feature area. The sheet does not say why it was redrawn. The timing follows the September sessions, but with no reason recorded I cannot claim those sessions caused it. In November we tested a Figma Make prototype and then a clickable React MVP.

## What the measurement plan never produced

Here is the part I would rather state than let a reader assume.

We wrote the thresholds. We never formally scored the transcripts against them. There is no table showing hypothesis one passing at 74% and hypothesis three failing at 31%. What I have is a set of qualitative patterns that happen to point where the thresholds anticipated, which is exactly the failure mode pre-registration is supposed to prevent.

So the honest scoreboard: the thresholds shaped what we asked and kept us from quietly reframing misses as findings. They did not produce a number. The cheap method produced four fixable defects in one session.

## The honest boundary

This was a course project with five people. The team produced 71 research and test records over three rounds (33 interviews, 28 think-aloud sessions and 10 MVP tests). I ran 14 of the 71 (7, 5 and 2 across the rounds) and owned the front-end component system and the shared state layer; the map, the wellness hub and the matching logic were other people's work.

The MVP runs on mock data. The backend was written and never connected, so matching, messaging and scheduling have never met a real record, a concurrent user or an empty state. No round recorded completion times or a fixed success criterion, so I can say the September navigation defect did not recur in the November task chain, and I cannot say by how much anything improved.

## The actual takeaway

Choosing a method is less about matching a technique to a question than about knowing what each one is blind to, and covering that blind spot with something else, often something much cheaper.

Set the pass mark in advance anyway. It disciplines the question even when it does not produce the number. But do not mistake the plan for the evidence: the clearest defects in this project came from one person, one prototype and the question *how do I go back?*
