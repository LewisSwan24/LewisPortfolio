## Why Accessibility Matters

When I started my Spectra Lens project—an AR tool for colorblind users—I realized that accessibility isn't an edge case. Approximately 8% of men and 0.5% of women have some form of color vision deficiency. That's hundreds of millions of people worldwide.

Designing for accessibility doesn't limit creativity; it expands it.

## Common Accessibility Barriers

### Visual Barriers

- Low color contrast
- Small text sizes
- Color-only information (like red/green status indicators)
- Missing alt text on images

### Motor Barriers

- Small touch targets
- Complex gestures required
- Time-limited interactions
- No keyboard navigation

### Cognitive Barriers

- Complex language
- Overwhelming information density
- Unpredictable navigation
- No error recovery options

## Practical Tips for Accessible Design

### 1. Start with Semantic Structure

Use proper heading hierarchy (H1 → H2 → H3) and semantic HTML elements. Screen readers rely on this structure to help users navigate.

### 2. Don't Rely on Color Alone

Always pair color with another indicator:
- Icons alongside colored status badges
- Patterns in addition to colors in charts
- Text labels with colored buttons

### 3. Design for Keyboard Navigation

Ensure all interactive elements are:
- Focusable with Tab key
- Activatable with Enter/Space
- Have visible focus indicators

### 4. Write Meaningful Alt Text

Good alt text describes the *purpose*, not just the appearance:

- **Poor**: "Image of a graph"
- **Better**: "Bar chart showing 40% increase in user engagement over 6 months"

### 5. Test with Real Users

No amount of automated testing replaces testing with people who use assistive technologies daily.

## Tools I Use

- **Contrast Checker**: WebAIM's contrast checker
- **Screen Readers**: VoiceOver (Mac), NVDA (Windows)
- **Accessibility Audits**: axe DevTools, Lighthouse
- **Color Blindness Simulators**: Sim Daltonism, Color Oracle

## The Business Case

Accessible design isn't just ethical—it's smart business:

- Larger potential user base
- Better SEO (screen reader-friendly = search engine-friendly)
- Reduced legal risk
- Improved usability for everyone

## Conclusion

Accessibility should be woven into your design process from day one, not bolted on at the end. When we design for the extremes, we often create better experiences for everyone.

The curb cut effect is real: features designed for wheelchair users benefit parents with strollers, travelers with luggage, and delivery workers with carts. The same principle applies to digital design.
