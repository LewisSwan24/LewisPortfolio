Accessibility work has a default shape: check the contrast, size the touch targets, write the alt text, never encode meaning in colour alone. That list is real and I use it. But on [Spectra Lens](/projects/spectra) — an AR tool that helps colourblind users read colour in the world — the requirement that changed the design most was not on any list I could have looked up.

It came out of an interview, from a second-year PhD student with deuteranopia:

> "The moment I pull out an app, the conversation stops. I'd rather guess than explain."

Nothing in WCAG covers that. It is not a perception problem. It is what using the fix costs you socially.

## What the interviews actually said

I ran semi-structured interviews across colour vision deficiency subtypes — deutan, protan, tritan — because the subtypes fail differently and I did not want to design for an average that describes nobody. Alongside that, a heuristic pass over existing colour-identification tools, and a co-design session mapping everyday colour moments.

Three things came back that a compliance checklist does not ask about.

**The decision window is two to three seconds.** Choosing a shirt, judging whether meat has turned, reading a status light. A tool slower than that loses to guessing — and people know it, so they do not open it twice.

**People want the answer, not the label.** Existing tools optimise for naming the hue precisely: "sage", "olive". Nobody in my interviews wanted the name. They wanted to know whether the food was still good. Naming is the part that is easy to build, which is why it is the part that got built.

**Most would rather be wrong than visibly assisted.** That is the quote above, and it was not one person. It reframed the project: the tool was competing not against other apps but against the option of quietly guessing.

## What that changed

**An overlay, not a filter.** The obvious build is a full-screen recolouring filter. I used a low-opacity annotation layer instead. A filter replaces the world; an overlay annotates it. The user is still looking at the real thing, and can hand the phone to someone or glance away without losing their place.

**Voice off by default.** This one came straight from the social-exposure finding. Spoken output is the accessible-by-default choice in most design systems, and here it was the feature people most wanted to be able to switch off. It ships silent, with voice as opt-in.

**One button. No account, no colour dictionary, no setup.** Point, get the decision.

**Say what to do, not what you are looking at.** "What you are looking at" is the phrasing that makes a tool feel like a diagnostic device, and a diagnostic device is exactly what nobody wanted to be seen holding.

## What testing changed again

Testing happened where the decisions happen — the canteen, a crossing, participants' own wardrobes. Two things surfaced only there.

The overlay opacity I had settled on obscured the texture of food, which is a cue people were already using. I lowered it, and added a haptic confirmation to carry the certainty the opacity had been carrying.

And in noisy environments, voice feedback was slower than simply reading the overlay — the reverse of the assumption that audio is the more accessible channel. Silent default, voice on request: confirmed by behaviour rather than by principle.

## The honest boundary

This was a university project. I recruited the participants myself, the sample was small, and there is no clinical validation behind the colour model. I can describe what these participants did in real settings and why each decision followed from it. I cannot tell you it generalises, and a portfolio that implies otherwise is selling something.

## What I took from it

Compliance rules are a floor, and a floor worth having — they encode failures the field has already paid for. But they describe properties of an *interface*, and the thing that decided this design was a property of the *situation*: who else is in the room when you need help.

You do not find that in a checklist. You find it by asking someone what it costs them to use the thing you built, and then believing the answer even when it contradicts a default you thought was settled.
