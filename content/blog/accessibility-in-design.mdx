Accessibility work has a default shape: check the contrast, size the touch targets, write the alt text, never encode meaning in colour alone. That list is real and I use it. But building Spectra Lens — an archived browser extension experiment that lets a reader change type and spacing on any page — taught me that the list is the part someone else already solved for you.

The hard part has no guideline. You are a guest on someone else's page, and every page fights you differently.

## What the guidelines did settle

WCAG's text-spacing criterion is unusually concrete. It names four properties — line height, letter spacing, word spacing, paragraph spacing — and gives thresholds a page should survive without breaking. That decided the control set. Without it I would have built a general CSS editor, which is a tool nobody wants to operate.

So the popup exposes those four, plus a typeface switch. Not because four felt like the right number, but because those four are the ones with published thresholds behind them.

## What the guidelines did not settle

**Injected styles lose by default.** A stylesheet added at the end still loses to an inline `style` attribute, and loses to a selector with higher specificity. Documentation sites were fine. News sites, with their layered themes, mostly ignored me.

**Single-page apps throw your work away.** Inject on load, navigate to another route, and the framework re-renders the subtree without your styles. The content script has to watch for route changes and re-apply — which no accessibility document mentions, because it is not an accessibility problem. It is a "you do not own this page" problem.

**Global settings are the wrong default.** Loose line height helps enormously on a dense documentation page. On a news site it just makes an article twice as long to scroll. A single global toggle means the reader is constantly turning the tool on and off, which is a good way to make them stop using it. Settings save per origin instead.

## The decision I most want to be asked about

The OpenDyslexic font ships inside the extension package rather than being fetched from a CDN at runtime.

A CDN would be smaller to distribute and easier to update. But a remote font request tells whoever hosts it which pages are being read, and it breaks the moment the host changes or the reader is offline. An accessibility tool that quietly reports your browsing to a third party is not one I want to have written.

The cost is real: the package is heavier, and updating the font means shipping a new version. I took it.

## The honest boundary

Nobody who needs this has used it.

Every control here is reasoned from a guideline or from watching a page break. That is enough to build something, and nowhere near enough to claim it helps. The obvious next step is the one I have not taken: give it to someone who reads this way and watch which controls they never touch. My guess is that at least one of the four is dead weight, and I would rather find that out than keep it because a specification mentions it.

It is a DEMO. It runs, the settings layer has tests, and it has never faced a store review or a site changing underneath it.

## What I took from it

Compliance rules are a floor, and a floor worth having — they encode failures the field has already paid for. But they describe properties of an *interface you control*. The moment your work has to survive on someone else's page, most of the difficulty sits somewhere the checklist never looks.
