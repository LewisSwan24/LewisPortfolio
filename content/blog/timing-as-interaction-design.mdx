Firmware engineers often treat debounce and cooldown intervals as electrical housekeeping. On an exhibition floor or a wrist screen, **timing is part of the interface**: an unfiltered reading can turn a clear physical rule into one that looks random.

## One ball pass, one event

In [MazeRunners](/projects/maze), ten FC-51 infrared sensors sat in the trap, ball-drop and finish zones of the acrylic maze. Their readings were sensitive to distance, angle and venue lighting, and they swung sharply. One Mega 2560 ran game state, the sensors, scoring, the LED strip and the finger-sleeve vibration, so a noisy reading reached every output at once.

A three-sample debounce and a 400 ms cooldown made one pass of the ball count once. The trade is a short blind window if a second event arrives inside the cooldown.

What the exhibition record shows is narrower than a rule about perception: players watched the ball and looked for their teammates, and some were startled by the vibration the first time it fired. My view that a mismatch between what players feel and what the score shows would cost their trust is a hypothesis; we did not test it.

## Windows sized for people

Timing also sets safety boundaries in [Walkie](/projects/walkie) and [On The Beat](/projects/onthebeat). Claude Code wrote most of the code for both; I set the rules, reviewed the output and tested them on the devices.

- **30-second physical pairing (`Walkie`)**: long enough to look at the watch and press the front key, short enough that a pairing request does not stay open on a desk.
- **Relay keep-alive (`Walkie`)**: the relay pings every 30 seconds. Gate L holds an idle link for 75 seconds, long enough to cover two pings, and it ran with a stand-in device through the relay, not with the watch. Whether the watch stays connected away from the cable has no run record yet.
- **Unmeasured wearable values (`On The Beat`)**: the 6-second wake window and the 1.5-second hold are in the firmware, but nobody has worn the bands at a gig, so both are estimates.
