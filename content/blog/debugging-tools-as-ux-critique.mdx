Most teams treat debugging utilities as disposable scaffolding hidden behind dev flags. Across a desktop AI agent, two hardware projects and this site, I have found inspectable tools more useful than that: they let a reviewer look at the rare states that static artboards and happy-path demos skip.

## Where an inspectable tool earns its place

1. **Lamplight Nook's recorded-event replay.** Live Claude SDK runs make rare budget-cap and stall states hard to reproduce on demand. [Lamplight Nook](/projects/lamplight) has a debug panel that replays anonymised recordings of real events, so those screens can be reviewed side by side. Claude Code wrote most of Lamplight's code; I set the rules, reviewed the output and used it myself.
2. **Protocol frame stepping for Walkie and MazeRunners.** The [Protocol Replay Studio](/studio/replay) steps through what happens when Walkie's USB cable is plugged in mid-turn (the turn in progress is dropped) and how MazeRunners' three-sample debounce and 400 ms cooldown turn one ball pass into one event. The frames are hand-authored simulations of that logic, not captured hardware traces, so they explain a rule rather than prove it.
3. **Reachability checks in the Lab FSM Explorer.** A transition diagram drawn on a whiteboard can look closed when it is not. The [Lab Workbench](/lab#fsm-explorer) runs a breadth-first check that flags any state with no path back to idle. I built it as a review aid; I have no record yet of it catching a real defect in one of my projects.

## Inspectability as design honesty

When an interface ships alongside its replay trace and the [Red-Team defense](/ai), reviewers do not have to take smooth screenshots on faith. They can step to the edge state themselves, as long as they are told which traces are simulated.
