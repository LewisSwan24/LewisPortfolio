Most social tools are tuned to stay on stage. They ask for feeds, avatars, mutual likes and persistent notifications long after two people could simply talk. In two physical-digital projects, [On The Beat](/projects/onthebeat) and [MazeRunners](/projects/maze), I worked from the opposite rule: technology should carry only enough structure to get past the first awkward moment, then step back once people are talking.

## The cost of "nearby radar" in social spaces

At a dark live gig, wanting to talk to someone three steps away comes with a real risk of losing face. Misreading the signal is awkward for both sides.

The common screen-first answer is a nearby-people radar: thumbnails, interests, match percentages, public prompts. My argument against it in a room built around live music, which is reasoning rather than a tested result:

1. **It replaces shared presence with silent browsing.** Heads tilt down into screens instead of catching eye contact when a song ends.
2. **It creates uneven exposure.** Names and bios on display ask people to perform before anyone has agreed to talk.

On [On The Beat](/projects/onthebeat), a teammate designed the original three cards (`SAY HI`, `FIRST SONG?`, `LET'S DANCE!`). I turned that concept into a running system with AI tools: Claude Code wrote the phone app, the wristband firmware and the relay, while I set the rules, reviewed the output and caught what was wrong, including a MEET screen that was only mocked data at first. Neither person learns the other's name until both say yes; only then does each see the other's first name and where to meet. When they end the meeting with LET'S FINISH, the card they met on goes off.

**Where the evidence stops:** on 25 September 2026 two wristbands showed MEET at the same time through the Fly.io relay. Nobody has worn them at a gig. The idea that the device should step back after a mutual yes is a design hypothesis waiting for field observation.

## Designing resistance into co-play

If On The Beat uses minimalism to lower the risk of a first greeting, [MazeRunners](/projects/maze) adds friction so players cannot play alone while standing side by side.

The first input idea was buttons and a joystick; the course paper records it was dropped as too much like an arcade game. Instead, each tilt direction is wired to a pair of same-coloured conductive wristbands (`A + B`, `B + C`, `C + D`, `D + A`), so no single player can move the ball alone. An early idea of small electric shocks as the penalty was dropped because we could not meet both the complexity and the safety requirements; trap events drive finger-sleeve vibration instead. That vibration turned out stronger than we expected, and some players were startled the first time it fired.

At the June 2026 exhibition, 9 groups (36 people) played and roughly 3 groups finished; the handwritten score sheet was not kept. A team member explained the rules first, and many players still struggled at first to match their colour to a move. The calls we noted were short ("wait", "stop", "left", "right"), and groups with a clear leader moved more smoothly.

| Design dimension | Typical screen-first reflex | Choice in these projects |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Icebreaker (`On The Beat`) | Nearby avatar radar and bio browsing | Names stay hidden until both people say yes |
| After the match (`On The Beat`) | Ongoing chat cards and badges | First name and meeting place shown, then LET'S FINISH turns the card off |
| Multiplayer steering (`MazeRunners`) | Buttons and a joystick (first idea, dropped) | Paired conductive wristbands, each direction needing two players |
| Penalty feedback (`MazeRunners`) | Electric shock (dropped: complexity and safety) | Relay-driven finger-sleeve vibration on debounced IR events |

## The withdrawal rule

Neither project has proved this rule in the field, but it is the one I design to:

- Let people get past the cold start without exposing profiles early.
- Keep physical rules on the body or the shared surface, not inside menus.
- Say plainly where bench tests end and outside observation still has to happen. You can try both state machines in the [Interaction Lab](/lab) and read their tradeoffs in the [AI Lab](/ai).
