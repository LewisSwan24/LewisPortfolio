## Overview

Spectra Lens is an augmented reality mobile application designed to assist individuals with color vision deficiency (colorblindness) in interpreting color-coded information in their daily lives. The app uses the device camera to detect colors and provide real-time audio and visual feedback.

This project was completed as part of my Master of Interaction Design coursework at UQ, spanning a full semester of research, design, and evaluation.

## Problem

Color vision deficiency affects approximately 8% of males and 0.5% of females globally. Many everyday tasks rely heavily on color coding:

- Traffic signals and signs
- Food freshness indicators
- Clothing coordination
- Data visualizations and charts
- Medication identification

Current solutions often require users to memorize workarounds or rely on others for assistance, impacting independence and confidence.

## Research

### Literature Review

I conducted an extensive review of existing accessibility technologies and color vision research, examining:

- Current color identification apps and their limitations
- Best practices in accessible design
- AR technology capabilities and constraints

### User Interviews

I interviewed 12 participants with various types of color vision deficiency:

- 8 with deuteranomaly (red-green)
- 3 with protanomaly (red-green)  
- 1 with tritanomaly (blue-yellow)

Key findings from interviews:

- Most frustration occurs in time-sensitive situations
- Existing apps feel "clinical" and stigmatizing
- Users want discrete, quick solutions

### Competitive Analysis

I analyzed 5 existing color identification apps, evaluating:

- Accuracy of color detection
- User interface design
- Accessibility features
- User reviews and pain points

## Insights

From my research, I identified three key insight areas:

### Speed Matters

Users need color information within 2-3 seconds to feel natural in social situations. Delayed responses create awkward pauses and draw unwanted attention.

### Context is Everything

Raw color names aren't always useful. Knowing something is "red" matters less than knowing if food is fresh or a traffic light means stop.

### Privacy Concerns

Many users don't want to publicly identify as colorblind. Solutions must be discrete and not draw attention.

## Ideation

I facilitated two co-design workshops with potential users to generate solution concepts:

### Workshop 1: Problem Mapping
Participants mapped their daily color-related challenges on journey maps, identifying critical pain points and existing coping strategies.

### Workshop 2: Solution Sketching
Using the "Crazy 8s" method, participants rapidly sketched potential solutions. Key themes emerged around:

- Quick-access camera features
- Audio feedback options
- Customizable color naming

### Concept Selection

Through dot voting and feasibility analysis, we selected the top 3 concepts for further development:

1. AR overlay with contextual labels
2. Discrete notification system
3. Smart color palette assistant

## Prototype

I created prototypes at three fidelity levels:

### Low-Fidelity
Paper prototypes for rapid concept testing, focusing on information architecture and core user flows.

### Mid-Fidelity
Wireframes in Figma exploring layout options, interaction patterns, and AR interface elements.

### High-Fidelity
Interactive prototype with:
- Realistic AR camera simulation
- Voice feedback integration
- Customizable accessibility settings
- Onboarding experience

## Testing

### Usability Testing

I conducted moderated usability tests with 8 participants using the high-fidelity prototype:

**Task Completion Rates:**
- Identify clothing colors: 100%
- Check traffic signal: 87.5%
- Read color-coded chart: 75%

**Key Issues Identified:**
- AR overlay sometimes obscured important details
- Voice feedback too slow in noisy environments
- Settings menu too complex

### Iteration

Based on testing feedback, I made the following improvements:

- Reduced overlay opacity by 20%
- Added haptic feedback option
- Simplified settings to 3 core options
- Introduced "quick mode" for common scenarios

## Outcome

The final design achieved:

- **92%** task success rate (up from 75%)
- **4.2/5** average satisfaction score
- **3.1 seconds** average time to color identification

### Impact

The project was selected for the UQ Design Showcase 2024 and received positive feedback from accessibility advocacy groups.

## Reflection

### What Worked Well

- Early and continuous user involvement
- Rapid prototyping enabled quick iteration
- Focus on real-world contexts over lab scenarios

### Lessons Learned

- AR interface design requires careful attention to cognitive load
- Accessibility features benefit all users, not just target demographics
- Co-design workshops generate richer insights than interviews alone

### Future Directions

If continuing this project, I would explore:

- Machine learning for contextual color interpretation
- Integration with smart glasses
- Community features for sharing color palettes
